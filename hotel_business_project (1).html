Investigate Hotel Business using Data Visualization

Understanding Booking & Cancellation Behaviour

Dataset: Hotel bookings, 2017–2019 (~119K rows × 29 columns)

Tools: Python (Pandas, NumPy), Matplotlib, Seaborn

Stage 0 — Problem Statement

1. Why does understanding customer booking behaviour matter for a hotel business?

A hotel's revenue depends on filling rooms at the right price, at the right time, with the right amount of certainty about who will actually show up. Understanding booking behaviour — which hotel type customers prefer, when demand rises and falls, and which bookings are most likely to be cancelled — lets a hotel move from reacting to bookings as they arrive, to actively managing demand. It can improve decisions such as:

  • Pricing — raising rates in high-demand months and discounting in low-demand months.
  • Staffing and inventory — scheduling more front-desk/housekeeping staff for peak months, and using quiet months for maintenance.
  • Overbooking strategy — deliberately overbooking rooms with historically high cancellation rates (e.g. long lead-time City Hotel bookings) to protect revenue from no-shows, without accidentally over-committing rooms with low cancellation risk.
  • Marketing spend — targeting promotions at the under-booked hotel type or at off-peak periods.
  • Cancellation policy design — tightening deposit or refund rules specifically for the booking patterns most associated with cancellation.

2. The three business questions, restated:

  • Hotel type popularity: Between the City Hotel and the Resort Hotel, which one do guests choose more often, and by how much?
  • Stay duration vs. cancellation: As a booking covers more nights, does it become more or less likely to be cancelled?
  • Lead time vs. cancellation: As a guest books further in advance of their stay, does that booking become more or less likely to be cancelled?

3. Objective

The objective of this project is to produce a clear, evidence-based analysis of booking and cancellation patterns across both hotel types, and to turn that analysis into a short set of concrete business recommendations. The main deliverable is this notebook: cleaned data, annotated visualisations answering all three business questions, and a final summary a non-technical hotel manager could act on.

Missing value decisions:

  • company (94.3% missing) — missing almost always means the booking was not made through a company, i.e. a leisure/individual guest rather than a corporate booking. Fill with 0 (a non-existent company ID) rather than dropping the column or the rows, since the absence itself is informative.
  • agent (13.7% missing) — missing means the booking was not placed through a travel agent (e.g. booked directly). Fill with 0 for the same reason as company.
  • children (4 rows, negligible) — almost certainly means 0 children were recorded rather than truly unknown, since babies and adults are always populated. Fill with 0.
  • city (0.4% missing) — the guest's home city. With no reliable way to infer it and no bearing on the three business questions, fill with 'Unknown' rather than dropping rows and losing other useful data in the same booking.

Q3 — What explains the seasonal pattern?

  • Holidays / leisure travel: Summer (June–August) is peak vacation season, driving demand for Resort Hotel especially, and school holidays concentrate family travel in this window.
  • Business travel: City Hotel's swing is larger and shifted slightly later (peaking into September–October), consistent with conference and trade-show season and the return of business travel after the summer lull, rather than tracking family holidays directly.
  • Winter trough: The Jan–March dip for both hotels reflects lower leisure travel after the December holidays and before spring, plus a slower business calendar in the new year.

Q4 — Operational/revenue decisions based on this trend:

  • Dynamic pricing: Raise room rates during the June–October peak; run discounts or value packages in the January–March trough to smooth demand and protect off-peak occupancy.
  • Staffing: Scale up front-desk, housekeeping, and F&B staff ahead of the peak season, and reduce fixed labour costs during the quiet months.
  • Maintenance scheduling: Use the Jan–March low-demand window for renovations, deep cleaning, and system upgrades, minimising disruption to guests during peak occupancy.

Q4 — Possible reasons longer stays are cancelled more often (City Hotel):

1. Higher total cost at stake — a longer stay means a larger total bill, so guests are more likely to shop around, find a better deal elsewhere, or cancel if travel plans change, since more money is on the line.

2. More speculative / flexible planning — longer city stays are often tied to business trips or multi-city itineraries booked before plans are fully firm, so guests hold provisional bookings and cancel or rebook as their schedule solidifies. Resort stays, by contrast, are usually planned as one deliberate vacation the guest is more committed to, regardless of length.

2.3 Impact of Lead Time on Cancellation Rate

Lead time, in my own words: Lead time is the number of days between the date a guest makes their booking and the date they are actually due to arrive at the hotel. A lead time of 0 means the guest booked and arrived the same day; a lead time of 300 means they booked about ten months ahead of their stay.

Q2 — Where is cancellation lowest / highest? Cancellation is lowest for last-minute bookings (0–7 days out) for both hotel types (10.5% City, 6.0% Resort). It is highest for City Hotel bookings made 365+ days in advance (52.8%) — the single highest cancellation rate seen anywhere in this analysis.

Q3 — Comparing the two hotel types: City Hotel rises steadily and steeply across every lead-time bucket, from 10.5% to 52.8%. Resort Hotel rises more gently through the middle buckets, then actually falls back down for the longest lead times (365+), dropping to 21.4% — lower than its own 181–365 day bucket. A likely explanation: very-far-ahead City Hotel bookings are often placeholders — unconfirmed business trips, conference dates that can shift, or flexible corporate travel policies — so they get cancelled or rebooked as plans firm up. Resort bookings made a year or more out, by contrast, tend to be a smaller, more deliberate group of guests (e.g. locking in a milestone trip or a known holiday window) who are more committed once booked, which is consistent with their cancellation rate easing off at the very longest lead times.

Stage 3 — Summary and Recommendations

1. Summary of key findings

  • Hotel type popularity: City Hotel is booked more often than Resort Hotel (≈61% vs. ≈39% of bookings, roughly 1.6x), with both hotels sharing a June–October demand peak and a January–March trough, though City Hotel's swing is larger and City Hotel's own peak leans slightly later (into autumn).
  • Stay duration vs. cancellation: City Hotel's cancellation rate rises sharply as length of stay increases; Resort Hotel's stays roughly flat regardless of stay length. Overall, City Hotel cancels noticeably more often than Resort Hotel.
  • Lead time vs. cancellation: Cancellation risk climbs steadily with lead time for both hotels, is lowest for last-minute bookings, and highest for City Hotel bookings made a year or more ahead (52.8%) — an effect far stronger than for Resort Hotel, whose cancellation rate actually eases off again at the very longest lead times.

2. Recommendations — hotel type and seasonality

  • Grow Resort Hotel's off-peak share by bundling multi-night leisure packages (spa, activities, family offers) specifically in the January–March trough, when both hotels are quiet and the resort has spare capacity to sell at a discount without cannibalising peak-season rates.
  • Shift some City Hotel marketing spend toward corporate/conference partnerships timed to the September–October peak, since that is when City Hotel demand is strongest and highest-value.

3. Recommendations — stay duration and cancellations

  • Introduce a partial deposit requirement that scales with stay length for City Hotel bookings (e.g. a higher deposit percentage for stays of 7+ nights), since long-stay cancellations are where City Hotel loses the most potential revenue.
  • Offer a modest non-refundable discount option for longer City Hotel stays, giving price-sensitive guests an incentive to commit in exchange for a lower rate, converting some of the "high cancellation risk" segment into locked-in revenue.

4. Recommendations — lead time and cancellations

  • Send automated confirmation reminders and re-engagement emails at set intervals (e.g. 6 months, 3 months, 1 month before arrival) for bookings made 180+ days out, prompting guests to confirm or reschedule rather than silently cancelling closer to the date.
  • Require a small, refundable-until-a-cutoff deposit on bookings placed more than a year in advance — particularly for City Hotel, where this segment shows the highest cancellation rate — to filter out speculative bookings while still protecting genuine early planners.

5. Highest-impact recommendation

The lead-time-based deposit policy for City Hotel is likely to have the single biggest impact. The data shows City Hotel's cancellation rate climbs to 52.8% for bookings made a year or more ahead — nearly 5x its cancellation rate for last-minute bookings — the single highest-risk segment identified anywhere in this analysis — while City Hotel also represents roughly 61% of all bookings, meaning this segment touches a large volume of revenue. A deposit requirement targeted specifically at this combination (City Hotel + long lead time) addresses the sharpest, highest-volume risk directly, rather than applying a blanket policy across all bookings regardless of actual cancellation risk.
